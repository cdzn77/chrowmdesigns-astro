"Make it feel more premium" can send a designer back to work without telling them what to change.

Imagine a hypothetical review of a booking page. The designer has brought two ways to explain the difference between room types. One reviewer asks for a darker background. Another wants a larger photograph. Both may have noticed something worth discussing, but the team still has not answered the question that brought the designs into the room: can a guest tell which room meets their needs?

That unanswered question matters more than the number of comments collected. A useful ChrowmDesigns review should leave a designer with a decision to explore or a concern to investigate. A board full of opinions is unfinished work.

## Give the review a boundary

Write the question beside the design before sending the link. For the booking example, it could be: "Does this comparison explain the practical difference between the standard and accessible rooms without making either sound like an upgrade?" That is a different review from deciding whether the photography fits the brand.

The [Department for Education's guidance on content reviews](https://design.education.gov.uk/content-design/quality-and-assurance) asks presenters to provide context and explain the feedback they need, including how people should submit it. That small amount of preparation makes the invitation more useful than an open request for thoughts.

Name what can change. If room names are still provisional, say so. If the reservation platform cannot display a second price column, show the constraint before someone spends the session recommending one. A constraint should explain a decision, not protect the design from scrutiny. Reviewers can still challenge whether it is real or worth accepting.

Give people enough context to judge the work without narrating every choice. A short description of the guest's task and the unresolved question usually offers a better starting point than a tour of the designer's process. Then let the page sit quietly for a moment. The first thing someone notices without explanation can be useful information.

## Separate the observation from the proposed fix

"Make the photograph larger" contains a proposed solution. Ask what prompted it.

Perhaps the reviewer cannot distinguish the room types because their images look identical. That points toward image selection. Perhaps the room photographs dominate the view already, while the distinguishing amenities disappear below them. Increasing the image size could deepen the problem. The same instruction can conceal opposite observations.

For this hypothetical review, a more useful note might read: "Both images show the bed from the same angle. I cannot see which room has space beside it for a wheelchair." The team can inspect that claim. A different photograph might help, or the information may need explicit text. The note leaves room for design judgment.

This does not mean every comment must arrive in a special format. People who commission design should not need a vocabulary lesson before raising a concern. The designer or facilitator can translate an ambiguous reaction into a question during the conversation. Ask which part prompted the reaction and what the reviewer expected to understand there.

Preferences deserve honest labels, too. "I prefer the darker version" is a legitimate preference. It becomes a stronger basis for a decision when connected to an agreed brand intention or a practical consequence. It is not, by itself, evidence that guests will book more often.

## Decide what happens to the comments

A review is not a vote on which suggestion wins. The team needs someone responsible for deciding what to explore, and a way to preserve a disagreement without implementing every proposed fix.

[Digital.gov's guidance on feedback](https://digital.gov/guides/hcd/design-concepts/feedback) describes revision as a separate part of the process and cautions against integrating every suggestion. It also recommends reaching beyond people already close to the project. Internal agreement can help a team move, but it cannot establish how an unfamiliar person will use the page.

In the booking example, the team could record a decision to replace the nearly identical room photographs and keep the current comparison structure for now. A separate question remains: can a guest find the accessibility details needed for their stay? That calls for evaluation with relevant participants, not another round of aesthetic preferences from the same room.

Keep the record short enough to revisit. Attach the observation to the version that prompted it, note the next action and identify who will take it. If the team chooses not to act, record the reason. "Not now because the comparison content is being rewritten" is more useful than a resolved comment with no explanation.

At the next review, bring back the question alongside the revision. Show what changed, then ask whether the original concern still exists. A larger image is easy to point to. Whether the guest now understands the room is the part worth checking.
