“Would you use this?” is an easy question to answer generously.

Imagine interviewing someone about a proposed appointment tool. They say reminders sound helpful. They prefer fewer steps. They agree that a simpler booking experience would be welcome. The conversation feels productive, but it has not explained how they booked their last appointment or why that process was difficult.

Ask for the last occasion instead.

The [GOV.UK guide to in-depth interviews](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews) recommends focusing on stories and real examples rather than general accounts of how things should happen. It changes the interview from a vote on a concept into an examination of behavior.

In the hypothetical appointment interview, ask where the person started. What information did they have ready? Did somebody else need to approve the time? What happened after they pressed the final button? Let the sequence unfold before introducing the proposed feature.

The detail that matters may be outside the website. Perhaps the participant needed to check a family member's schedule. Perhaps they photographed a confirmation screen because they did not trust the email to arrive. Neither detail automatically proves a feature is needed. Both give the team something concrete to investigate.

Avoid converting each anecdote into a requirement during the session. Listening while silently building a solution can make the interviewer hurry past evidence that contradicts the emerging idea. Keep a separate place for interpretations and return to them afterward.

A practical note format has two columns: what the participant said or did, and what the team thinks it might mean. Quote accurately when retaining a quotation, with consent and appropriate handling of personal information. A paraphrase belongs in a different form.

ChrowmDesigns can use that distinction in a project discussion without pretending that an interview has settled market demand. Research narrows uncertainty. It does not remove the need for judgment.

There is also a recruitment question. A colleague who understands the product's vocabulary may tell a coherent story that bears little resemblance to a new customer's experience. Choose participants whose circumstances matter to the decision. Record who is absent from the sample.

Afterward, look for a claim that would change the design. “People want convenience” is too broad to guide a screen. “Participants needed to compare the proposed time with a schedule outside the application” suggests a specific interruption the design should tolerate.

A good follow-up can be quieter than the prepared question. Ask what happened next, then leave enough room for the answer. The awkward pause may contain the part that a polished pitch would have talked over.
