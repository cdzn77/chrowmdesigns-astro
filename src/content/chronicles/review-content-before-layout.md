A page can look finished while its most important design decisions remain unresolved. The headline fits, the cards align and the button sits comfortably above the fold. Then the actual content arrives, and the page has to explain a service instead of displaying a composition.

Placeholder text is useful for a quick structural sketch. Trouble starts when that sketch becomes the basis for approval. At ChrowmDesigns, the useful review question is whether the page helps someone understand an offer and take the intended next step. A layout filled with anonymous text cannot answer it.

Consider a hypothetical booking service. Its prototype has a short headline, a single sentence and a button labeled "Get started." The real offer needs to explain who can book, where the service operates and whether the first appointment costs money. None of those details is decoration. Each can change a visitor's decision.

## Make the draft representative

The writer does not need to produce a polished campaign before the designer can begin. A rough paragraph that explains the actual offer is enough to expose many of the constraints. If eligibility needs explaining, put that explanation into the prototype. If the next step is a consultation request, say so on the button.

Representative content means plausible meaning as well as plausible length. Filling a card with a long string of unrelated words tests wrapping. It does not test whether the heading distinguishes that service from the card beside it. Both questions matter, and they need different checks.

[GOV.UK's prototyping guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/making-prototypes) describes prototypes as a way to explore and test designs before committing to a build. Applying that principle to content means letting the words expose problems early, while the structure is still easy to change. The specific review method here is our recommendation, rather than a prescribed GOV.UK workflow.

For the booking example, an early draft might explain the appointment length and what the visitor should prepare. Review it in the actual page. If it overwhelms the introduction, decide what belongs there and what belongs near the booking action. Shortening it without that decision can remove information the visitor needs.

## Give unfinished content a named owner

A missing paragraph needs a person responsible for supplying it, a clear brief and a review date. A department name leaves room for everyone to assume somebody else is writing it.

The brief should explain what the reader needs to know, where the text appears and what happens next. Share the narrow layout as well as the desktop view. An approximate length can help the writer understand the available space, but it should remain a design constraint to discuss, not an excuse to omit a necessary qualification.

Keep the content status visible in the project record. Drafted, fact-checked and approved mean different things. A designer may have written a useful working version without having the authority to confirm a price or a service promise. Mark those questions explicitly so an attractive prototype does not accidentally settle them.

## Review the page again when the words change

Replacing a four-word headline with a sentence changes the composition. That is a reason to review the page again. It may need more space, a different image crop or a revised order of information.

Start at phone width and read through the complete task. Check whether the heading introduces the content beneath it, whether the action label describes the next step, and whether the supporting explanation appears before the visitor needs it. Then check wider views. A page that works at one width has only passed one part of the review.

Avoid shrinking the type simply to preserve the approved screenshot. The screenshot recorded a different content state. Preserve the reader's ability to understand the page, and discuss the resulting layout change with whoever approved the earlier version.

The small text deserves the same attention. Form hints, captions, empty states and confirmation messages often survive outside the main review. In the booking example, the confirmation should explain whether an appointment is reserved or a request is awaiting a reply. A polished headline cannot compensate for uncertainty at the end of the task.

## Make the launch decision explicit

Before publication, search the source for known placeholder strings and inspect the rendered pages. Searching catches familiar markers; reading catches plausible text that was never approved. Include social descriptions and other metadata in that review.

For every unfinished section, decide whether to complete it, remove it or defer the release. Record the owner and the consequence. A blank space in a checklist is too easy to mistake for someone else's responsibility.

Approval should identify what was reviewed: the content version, the page and the relevant screen sizes. When those change substantially, the approval needs another look. This keeps the final conversation practical. The team can point to the words a visitor will read and decide whether they are ready to publish.
