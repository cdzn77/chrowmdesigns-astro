A green dot cannot tell everyone that an item is ready.

Imagine a hypothetical production dashboard using green for approved files and red for files needing revision. The color coding is familiar. It also asks the user to perceive and remember a difference that may be unavailable in their viewing conditions.

Add the status in words, and the dot becomes support rather than the entire message.

[WCAG's guidance on use of color](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html) explains that color should not be the only visual means of conveying information or prompting a response. That principle predates the newest interface trends. It is especially relevant when a minimal design removes labels in pursuit of a cleaner screen.

The correction does not need to make the interface visually loud. A short label such as “Needs review” can sit beside the colored marker. A shape may reinforce a distinction where words would be awkward, provided its meaning is understandable in context.

The same issue appears in editorial charts. If two lines are distinguished only by color, a legend does not necessarily solve the problem. A direct label or a different line treatment can make the relationship clearer. The right choice depends on what the reader needs to compare.

For ChrowmDesigns, this is an opportunity to strengthen the visual language. A useful system gives important meaning more than one expression without making every element compete for attention.

Do not confuse this with a contrast review. A label can have excellent contrast and still rely entirely on color to explain its status. Conversely, adding words does not fix text that is too faint to read. These are separate questions, and both need attention.

A quick grayscale review can expose dependencies, but it is not a complete accessibility test. Use it as a design prompt. Ask what disappears when the hue difference disappears, then inspect whether the remaining form communicates the necessary information.

Keep the meaning consistent across pages. If an outlined circle indicates pending work in one area and a completed step in another, the interface has created a memory problem. A color change cannot repair inconsistent semantics.

The most useful place to document the rule is with the component. Show the label, icon or shape that accompanies each state, including the state that rarely appears in the polished mockup.

Minimalism is not measured by how few marks survive on screen. A status that requires a second person to explain it has removed something the design needed.
