The same photograph can need different alternative text on two pages.

On one page, it may show the product being discussed. On another, it may be a decorative background behind a heading that already carries the message. Describing every visible object in both places would ignore why the image is there.

The [W3C alternative-text decision tree](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/decision-tree/) makes context central to the choice. It distinguishes informative images from functional or decorative ones. That is a more useful starting point than a blanket instruction to write a description for every file.

Consider a hypothetical portfolio cover showing a ticket-booking interface. If the surrounding text already names the project and the image links to its case study, the accessible name should make the destination clear without repeating a long visual inventory. Inside the case study, a detailed screenshot may need a nearby explanation of the interaction being demonstrated.

The image has changed jobs.

For an abstract Chronicles cover, the relevant description can be brief. A reader may benefit from knowing that a mirror reflects a forest in an otherwise empty desert. They do not necessarily need every hue and shadow unless that contrast is part of the article's meaning.

ChrowmDesigns uses conceptual imagery to establish tone and suggest a question. It should not require somebody to interpret an unlabeled metaphor to understand the article itself. The argument belongs in readable text.

A complex diagram asks for more care. A short alternative cannot always carry the relationships shown in a process map. Provide an explanation in the surrounding content, or another suitable text representation, so the reader can access the information rather than just hear that a diagram exists.

Write alternatives after deciding the page's content structure. Asset libraries can store a useful starting description, but the final wording depends on use. Reusing the same image does not require reusing the same alternative.

Review functional images as controls. If a graphic acts as a link or button, a description of its appearance may fail to explain the action. “Black arrow” tells someone less than the destination or operation represented by that arrow.

Avoid adding unsupported interpretation. A portrait does not establish that its subject is confident, frustrated, or a particular kind of professional. Describe what is visible and relevant, unless the page supplies the missing context.

The last check is editorial: read the page's text with the image descriptions in place. If the sequence repeats itself or loses its point, adjust it. Alternative text is part of the reading experience, not a separate box to fill after publishing.
