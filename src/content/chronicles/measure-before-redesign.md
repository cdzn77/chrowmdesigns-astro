A new homepage cannot repair a service nobody understands.

Consider a hypothetical studio receiving the wrong kinds of inquiries. Its owner asks for a more impressive website. The existing page may look dated, but the more useful question is what visitors believe the studio actually sells. If the page presents production support as full-service strategy, a cleaner layout could make the wrong promise more convincing.

That is a scope problem before it is a visual problem.

The [GOV.UK discovery guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-discovery-phase-works) treats discovery as a period for understanding the problem and its constraints before committing to a solution. That distinction was established well before the current enthusiasm for rapid website generation. It remains useful when a fresh interface can be produced faster than a team can agree on its purpose.

For a small business, discovery need not become a month of workshops. Start with one page and one action. Write down what a visitor should understand before taking that action. Then compare that expectation with the page's actual words, the questions arriving by email, and a few observed attempts to use it.

Keep those types of evidence separate. An analytics exit does not explain why someone left. A founder's concern does not establish that customers share it. A usability session can reveal confusion without estimating how widespread that confusion is.

A useful brief might say: prospective clients cannot tell whether implementation is included in the quoted service. That statement points toward a testable change. “Make the site feel premium” leaves the team debating typography while the unanswered question survives.

The first proposed intervention could be two sentences near the inquiry link, supported by a clearer service outline. Test that small change before replacing the entire information structure. A redesign may still be justified. The point is to know what it must preserve and what it must correct.

At ChrowmDesigns, that is the position this series takes on redesign work: define the decision the visitor is struggling to make, then decide how much design is needed to support it.

There is a budget consequence. If the problem is unclear positioning, the project needs access to the people who define the offer. If the issue is a broken form, it needs technical diagnosis. If visitors understand the offer but do not trust it, the missing material may be evidence rather than another layout.

Before commissioning the work, ask the team to finish one sentence: after this change, a visitor should be able to do something they cannot reliably do now. If the answer only describes how the page will look, the brief is still missing its most useful line.
