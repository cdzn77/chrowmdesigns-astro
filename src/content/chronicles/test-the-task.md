A participant can finish your prototype and still teach you almost nothing.

Suppose the task says, “Click Services, select the audit package, and submit an inquiry.” The participant follows it successfully. That confirms the controls can be operated under instruction. It says much less about whether someone could recognize the right service without those instructions.

The task has supplied the design's missing explanation.

The [GOV.UK guidance on moderated usability testing](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing) centers sessions on observing people attempting relevant activities with a service or prototype. For this retrospective, the useful lesson is the difference between an outcome and a route. A test can name what someone needs without naming the labels they should click.

For a hypothetical studio website, a better situation might be: your team has a working product, but customers keep getting stuck during signup. Find out whether this studio offers an appropriate kind of help and what you would need before contacting it.

Now the participant must interpret the offer. They may choose a service the team did not expect. They may look for evidence of comparable work or stop because the page never explains the first engagement. Those observations can matter more than the elapsed time.

Decide in advance what the session needs to reveal. If the question concerns terminology, a simple prototype may be enough. If the question concerns form recovery or keyboard interaction, the prototype must support those behaviors. A clickable picture cannot prove an implemented form is accessible.

During the session, resist rescuing every hesitation. A prompt such as “What are you looking for?” can reveal intent without exposing the correct answer. Telling someone where to click turns the rest of the task into a guided tour.

Record the intervention. A completed task after moderator help is different from an independent completion, even if both reach the final screen. That distinction should survive the report.

A useful research report identifies who participated and which version they tested. That gives a ChrowmDesigns project discussion something firmer than a collection of reactions.

A useful finding connects an observation to a consequence. “The navigation was confusing” is weak. “The participant opened three categories because none described support for an existing product” is actionable. It gives the team language to revisit and a behavior to test again.

Do not turn a small round into a percentage that sounds like a population estimate. Three observed failures are three cases to understand. Their value lies in the mechanism they expose.

Before the next session, read the task aloud and underline every phrase copied from the interface. Those phrases may be doing the work that the design is supposed to do.
